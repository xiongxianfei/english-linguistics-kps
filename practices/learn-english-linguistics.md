# Learn English linguistics

## Key takeaway
Learn to explain and investigate English examples by connecting established linguistic distinctions, their conditions and the evidence for an analysis.

## Summary
Choose a linguistic question, study a worked explanation, analyze a new case and check both the reasoning and its limits. This draft provides a broad learning route and one complete paper exercise on sound contrast and contextual realization. Other areas are mapped for later learning units; the route is an authored application of established knowledge, with no demonstrated learning outcome.

## Target and prerequisites

The target is understanding how English works: describe an example, connect its relevant properties, explain an analysis, and identify evidence that could change it. This is distinct from demonstrating conversational fluency or reproducing a pronunciation. Begin with ordinary English reading ability; necessary sound notation is introduced below. A notebook is sufficient for the paper exercise. Recordings are needed only for claims about an actual spoken token.

Stage order is recommended; prerequisites and available evidence govern entry. Revisit a stage whenever an unresolved distinction blocks the next task.

The learning route selects established distinctions from Anderson's inspected articulation, transcription, phonological contrast and word-structure passages. The [source record](../references/anderson-2018-essentials-of-linguistics.md) gives their locations and limits. Our grouping, task sequence and feedback are authored choices. The shared [subject map](../docs/subject-map.md#sound-systems) identifies thirteen sound topics and their relationships, existing explanations and source limits. This Practice selects from that structure; its learning route is not a claim that the textbook endorses this curriculum. New examples follow the package's General American convention.

## Stage1 Choose an analysis target

1. Write one question and the material available. For this first exercise use: “How can a sound difference distinguish words in one comparison but reflect context in another?” The supplied material is written examples and a stated pattern, not recordings.
2. Set an output: a table separating sound category, environment and predicted realization, followed by a short explanation of what the comparison supports.
3. Record the variety and evidence boundary. Use General American for this exercise. Mark all bracketed forms below as illustrative predictions. If your question concerns a particular speaker, obtain an appropriate recording or retain a prediction-only result.

An adequate target names an answerable distinction. “Learn all phonetics” needs a smaller question before proceeding. Keep personal responses and recordings outside the public knowledge package.

## Stage2 Explain a sound contrast

1. Read the notation and worked comparison below. Explain the difference between a category and its realization before interpreting the table.
2. Cover the worked conclusion and explain why the two comparisons answer different questions. Identify the properties and conditions used, not just the resulting symbols.
3. Attempt the new case before reading its feedback. Record a prediction, its reason and one unsupported conclusion.
4. Compare your answer with the feedback. Correct the reasoning at the point it diverges. If you need to inspect an actual voice, use a recording and retain uncertainty where cues do not decide the analysis.

### Representation and necessary distinctions

Angle brackets such as <pin> indicate spelling; /p/ denotes a phoneme, a contrastive sound category in the analysis; [pʰ] denotes a possible phonetic realization. An allophone is a realization associated with a phoneme. Different phonetic details do not automatically establish different phonemes. The [representation Concept](../concepts/representation-levels.md) and [phone and phoneme Concept](../concepts/phone-phoneme-and-allophone.md) deepen these distinctions.

For consonants, place locates the constriction, manner describes how airflow is constrained, and voicing concerns vocal-fold vibration. In the selected categories, [p] and [b] are bilabial stops: lips close and release. Their voicing categories differ. [t] is an alveolar voiceless stop; [s] is an alveolar voiceless fricative, with a narrow passage rather than complete closure. The [articulation Concept](../concepts/consonant-articulation.md) and Phonetics Model explain these properties further.

Aspiration is voiceless airflow after stop release, marked [ʰ]. For this exercise, English /p t k/ commonly show it at a stressed syllable's beginning; a preceding /s/ in the same onset changes that expectation. The onset is the consonant material before the syllable nucleus; stress is relative syllabic prominence. These conditions matter together. This bounded pattern does not specify final stops or every accent and speaking style. [Anderson's aspiration passage](../references/anderson-2018-essentials-of-linguistics.md#aspiration) supplies the source basis.

### Worked comparison

| Supplied example | Target and condition | Predicted target phone |
|---|---|---|
| *pin* /pɪn/ | /p/ at the start of a stressed syllable | [pʰ] |
| *spin* /spɪn/ | /p/ after /s/ within the stressed onset | [p], with less aspiration |
| *bin* /bɪn/ | Different phoneme in the matched environment | [b] as a category label, not a measured voicing trace |

**Interpretation:** *Pin/bin* illustrates a lexical contrast. *Pin/spin* changes the environment as well as the word, so it is not a minimal pair differing only in aspiration. The Model relates category and environment to realization. The observed aspiration of any particular speaker remains unknown.

### New case and feedback

Before reading the feedback, consider the target /t/ in *top* and *stop*, each used as a stressed one-syllable word. Predict only that target phone. Explain whether the same rule should assign aspiration to /s/. Then assess this claim: “If I cannot hear a release clearly, the stop was deleted.”

**Feedback:** The bounded pattern predicts [tʰ] in *top* and [t] with less aspiration after /s/ in *stop*. Changing place from bilabial /p/ to alveolar /t/ preserves membership in the voiceless-stop class. /s/ is a fricative, so voicelessness alone does not put it in that class. A difficult-to-hear release does not establish deletion: a retained closure or overlapping cues may require further investigation. **Elision** means a segment is absent from the reduced analysis; **reduction** need not remove it. These distinctions recur in the [Connected Speech Concept](../concepts/connected-speech.md) and the Phonetics Model because each explanation needs them locally.

| Your response | Next action |
|---|---|
| Correct targets, class and conditions; predictions distinguished from observations | Continue and record the limited result |
| Correct symbols but no reason, or /s/ treated as a stop | Revisit manner and the rule's target class, then explain again |
| Every voiceless stop claimed to behave identically | Revisit the two onset environments and retain the stated scope |
| An exact timing or observed deletion claimed without evidence | Remove the unsupported observation and state what data are needed |

## Stage3 Plan the next linguistic question

1. Choose a next question from the learning route or shared subject map, based on an unresolved distinction in your answer. The task here is selecting a next investigation; the other areas remain lesson outlines.
2. Write the example, proposed question and missing evidence. For instance, with *Those students repaired the printers. They tested them again.*, ask which group *they* refers to. One candidate is the students; another previously mentioned plural group could be available in a wider context. The written pair alone does not settle every possible context.
3. Identify the established account and existing explanation to inspect. Record the source locator, its scope and which relationship will help answer the question. If the needed passage has not been inspected, record a support gap rather than copying a confident conclusion.
4. Specify a new comparison and its feedback criterion before drafting another unit. For the reference question, supply two explicit contexts and ask which candidate each makes available. Keep the contextual assumption visible when comparing answers.

The output is one bounded learning plan with support and gaps. Completing this planning stage does not demonstrate mastery of syntax, semantics or discourse.

## Stage4 Check understanding and record uncertainty

1. Close the explanation and restate the distinction, conditions and reasoning for the new sound case. Compare your response with the feedback; record whether you used hints.
2. Separate an explanation you can give from an empirical claim you have checked. A successful paper prediction does not demonstrate listening accuracy or production skill.
3. Record the task, source and document version, actual response, correction and remaining uncertainty. Choose to continue, repeat the relevant explanation or obtain missing evidence. Do not report improvement without a recorded comparison.
