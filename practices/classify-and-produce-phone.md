# Classify and produce a phone

## Key takeaway
Identify what the input represents, build a justified articulatory description, then listen and attempt the sound without confusing a chart label, a phoneme or an observed token.

## Summary
This Practice combines phone classification with listening, articulation, production and evidence checking. It yields a **phone classification record** with the input and its context, a proposed phonetic symbol, vowel/consonant analysis, articulatory features, source support, observed versus predicted properties, actual attempts and uncertainty. General American supplies the English reference contexts; the IPA chart itself is cross-language. The six stages give a recommended route, not compulsory prerequisites in every case. Symbol-led analysis and recording-led investigation converge on a revisable record, not on a claim that every input has one certain answer.

## Purpose and prerequisites
Use this when a symbol, a sound within a word, or a recording prompts a question such as “Is this a vowel or consonant; is it voiced; where and how is it produced?” Have the exact input, its source, and an explicit transcription level. For audio, retain a timestamp and enough surrounding speech to locate the target. Have playback and a way to record your own attempt or obtain live feedback. No programming, acoustic software or paid pronunciation service is required. Specialist analysis may be needed when listening cannot distinguish alternatives.

Start with **what is actually available**. A printed `[z]` allows classification of an IPA phone category but does not establish that a recorded speaker produced `[z]`. A broad `/r/` from an English dictionary is not a command to produce the IPA trill `[r]`. With no audio or demonstration, theoretical classification may be recorded; listening and production remain pending. Do not claim an exact narrow transcription from spelling alone.

Practice with comfortable articulatory movements. Stop on discomfort, do not press on the throat, and do not force the tongue to match a drawing. Private recordings and learner observations belong outside the public repository; obtain consent for another person's voice. Link to external media instead of copying it.

## Essential local model
A **phoneme** is a contrastive category in a language analysis, written `/.../`; a **phone** is a phonetic type or realization, written `[...]`. An **allophone** relates a phone to a phoneme in a particular system. Symbols are representations, not the physical sounds. A single phoneme may have several phones, and classification of a phone alone does not establish phonemic status.

For the intended consonant branch, describe **voicing, place and manner** plus relevant secondary features. For a vowel, describe **height, backness and rounding**, while recording phonation, nasalization, rhoticity and movement when relevant. Do not force the same fields onto both branches. IPA chart position tells what a symbol conventionally denotes; it is not a measurement of every General American token using a similar phonemic label.

## Stage1 Identify the input and evidence
**Goal:** Establish whether you have spelling, a phonemic label, a phonetic transcription, or actual audio.

**Why:** These inputs support different conclusions; the letter `s` and the phone `[s]` are not interchangeable.

**What to do:** Copy the exact input and notation, record provenance and context, and separate expected properties from observations.

**What to observe:** Missing diacritics, ambiguous slash/bracket use, dictionary shorthand and a recording without a stable target interval.

**Success signal:** The record identifies the level of evidence and says what has not yet been observed.

**Next:** Locate the target; obtain lexical or audio context before making a stronger claim.

**Understanding and procedure**
For an orthographic input such as `<th>`, first identify a word and candidate pronunciation; spelling may represent different phonemes. For a broad `/p/`, state the English phoneme but do not assume aspiration. For `[pʰ]`, copy both the base symbol and superscript aspiration mark. `[ph]` is not the same transcription. For audio, listen to the whole word or phrase and mark a candidate interval; do not crop away transitions before identifying it.

Create four small fields: **source description**, **prediction**, **heard observation**, and **measurement**. Leave unperformed measurements blank. An unheard chart recording is not heard evidence; an instructor's articulatory description is a source claim, not a view inside the learner's vocal tract.

**Decision points and fallback**
If you cannot determine whether a source uses broad /r/ as a convention or narrow [r] for a trill, consult its notation key. If the target is an unexplained letter or isolated noisy audio, keep the analysis unresolved rather than selecting a plausible label and calling it a fact.

## Stage2 Locate the target and choose a route
**Goal:** Determine the target unit and whether a vowel, consonant, sequence or unresolved analysis is appropriate.

**Why:** A vowel-like approximant, a syllabic consonant and a diphthong defeat a simple letter-count or syllable-count rule.

**What to do:** Use the symbol's chart location and contextual evidence, or form competing audio hypotheses when no trustworthy transcription exists.

**What to observe:** Affricates, vowel movement, coarticulation, syllabicity and symbols that modify another symbol.

**Success signal:** The choice of branch and target span is justified, including any uncertainty.

**Next:** Describe features; collect better evidence if no stable target can be identified.

**Understanding and procedure**
**Symbol-led route:** locate the base symbol in the official chart, retaining diacritics and tie bars. Check the consonant, vowel and other-symbol panels rather than guessing from an English letter. `[t͡ʃ]` is an affricate transcription with coordinated closure and fricated release; two printed symbols do not require two unrelated phones. A diphthong such as illustrative `[aɪ]` describes a vowel movement and should not be assigned one fixed tongue position for its entire duration.

**Audio-led route:** listen in context and propose a broad category from sustained resonance, friction, closure/release or a nasal quality. These are cues, not guaranteed classifications. Write competing labels when appropriate and compare with a suitable reference. Avoid pretending ordinary listening can uniquely determine every place of articulation or laryngeal detail.

General American `/j/` and `/w/` are consonantal glides in ordinary phonological descriptions although their articulation is vowel-like. Syllabic `[n̩]` remains a nasal consonant even when it is a syllable nucleus. A reduced vowel can also have altered phonation. “It is in the nucleus” and “it is voiced” are therefore not sufficient vowel tests.

**Decision points and fallback**
If the input is a sequence, classify its components and their coordination rather than forcing it into a single fixed feature bundle. A non-English phone can be classified with the IPA while remaining outside the ordinary GA inventory. Retain “unresolved” when the audio does not support a narrower category.

## Stage3 Build the articulatory feature description
**Goal:** Give a coherent feature bundle and distinguish the chart category from a particular production.

**Why:** Voicing, constriction and lip/tongue configuration are different dimensions; one label cannot stand in for all of them.

**What to do:** Follow the consonant or vowel branch and annotate diacritics separately.

**What to observe:** Missing nasality, aspiration confused with voicing, vowel rounding guessed from height, and context-specific features treated as permanent.

**Success signal:** Each claimed feature has a chart, source, observation or explicitly inferred basis.

**Next:** Listen and reproduce a contrast; revisit the input if the feature description is inconsistent.

**Understanding and procedure**
For **consonants**, read the place column and manner row, then the voicing position or diacritic. On the pulmonic chart, paired positions distinguish voiceless on the left and voiced on the right. Bilabial involves both lips; labiodental the lower lip and upper teeth; dental the teeth region; alveolar the ridge behind the upper teeth; velar the tongue back and soft palate. Stops have oral closure, fricatives a turbulent constriction, nasals an oral closure with nasal airflow, approximants a more open constriction, and lateral approximants airflow around the tongue's sides. These are introductory descriptions, not acoustic tests.

For **vowels**, read height (close through open), backness (front, central, back) and rounding. At paired positions on the vowel chart the right-hand symbol is rounded; this is **not the consonant chart's voicing rule**. Track a diphthong's movement instead of forcing a static label. The chart category `[u]` is close back rounded, but the English GOOSE phoneme `/u/` need not have that exact position in every GA realization.

| Symbol used as a phone category | Introductory classification |
|---|---|
| `[p]` | Voiceless bilabial stop |
| `[pʰ]` | Voiceless bilabial stop with aspiration |
| `[z]` | Voiced alveolar fricative, not dental |
| `[m]` | Voiced bilabial nasal |
| `[ŋ]` | Voiced velar nasal, not approximant |
| `[ɾ]` | Voiced alveolar tap or flap |
| `[i]` | Close front unrounded vowel |
| `[ɪ]` | Near-close near-front unrounded vowel |
| `[æ]` | Near-open front unrounded vowel |
| `[u]` | Close back rounded vowel |
| `[ə]` | Mid central vowel; neutral or unrounded is the working English description here |

These chart-based examples are symbol classifications, not measurements or a complete GA phoneme inventory. The [official chart record](../sources/international-phonetic-association-undated-ipa-chart.md) supports the basic categories. The [diacritics and other symbols record](../sources/international-phonetic-association-undated-diacritics-and-other-symbols.md) supports the modifiers and special cases.

A voiceless diacritic changes a plain vowel description: `[i̥]` must not be called voiced merely because it is a vowel. `[n̩]` marks syllabicity, not a change to vowel manner. Aspiration `[ʰ]`, nasalization and rhoticity supply different information. Schwa is often unstressed in the English system, but stress is a contextual syllable property, not a universal definition of the phone `[ə]`.

**Decision points and fallback**
`/r/` in an English broad source can stand for a rhotic category; the IPA phone `[r]` is a trill, while `[ɹ]` is an approximant symbol and GA rhotics have further variation. Consult the convention and evidence before choosing a phone. Do not infer allophony merely from similarity or from failure to find a minimal pair.

## Stage4 Listen reproduce and compare contrasts
**Goal:** Connect the feature description to an audible target and an attempted gesture.

**Why:** Reading “voiced alveolar fricative” does not demonstrate hearing or producing one.

**What to do:** Listen to an appropriate labeled reference, attempt the gesture comfortably, and compare a nearby contrast.

**What to observe:** What is actually audible or visible, what is only inferred, and what changes when one feature is varied.

**Success signal:** A private record contains an inspected reference and an actual attempt, with observations rather than an assumed match.

**Next:** Compare with feedback; retain listening or production as pending if either has not happened.

**Understanding and procedure**
Use the interactive IPA chart for a general phone demonstration and Sounds American for an American teaching context. These are different reference purposes: an isolated chart sound is not necessarily the contextual allophone in an English word. Record the exact lesson or chart symbol and what you actually listened to. Confirm audio works in your environment rather than counting a static page as a listening task.

For `[z]`, compare `[s]` and `[z]`: aim for a similar alveolar fricative constriction while changing laryngeal behavior. A sustained `[z]` may produce a felt vibration; a light touch is optional and **cannot alone verify voicing**, especially in brief or partly devoiced sounds. Do not press on the throat. Keep the gesture short and comfortable; do not append an unintended vowel merely to say the consonant.

For `[i]` versus `[u]`, compare tongue/lip settings as well as the sound; a mirror can show rounding but cannot directly establish tongue height. To explore `[ŋ]`, begin from an appropriate *sing* model and attend to the final nasal without assuming a following `[g]`. Articulation practice in isolation does not establish that `[ŋ]` is licensed word-initially in ordinary GA words.

Record one baseline and a later attempt, or obtain live feedback. A sustained fricative/nasal can aid observation, but a stop and a tap should not be stretched into a different manner. Return from deliberate articulation to a natural syllable or word context.

**Decision points and fallback**
If the target cannot be reproduced comfortably, stop production and continue analysis or listening only. Never invent the missing attempt. If another phone sounds plausible, record both hypotheses; physical imitation by itself does not prove which sound the source speaker produced.

## Stage5 Verify the classification and production
**Goal:** Test the analysis and the attempted sound with evidence appropriate to each claim.

**Why:** Symbol lookup, perceptual identification and successful production are three different achievements.

**What to do:** Check the label against the chart, test a labeled contrast without looking, and compare the recorded production or obtain informed feedback.

**What to observe:** Confusions, dependence on visual labels, recording limitations and disagreements between prediction and evidence.

**Success signal:** The conclusion reports supported features and unresolved ones separately; no absent check is labeled passed.

**Next:** Revise the uncertain feature, obtain a better sample, or retain a bounded conclusion.

**Understanding and procedure**
1. **Classification check:** explain the proposed feature bundle before revealing the source's description. Verify row/column, modifiers and notation convention. This checks category knowledge, not recognition of an unknown token.
2. **Perception check:** ask a partner to shuffle a short set of known references, or use a task with answers hidden until after your decision. Identify the target versus the stated contrast and log the actual responses. Known alternation, spelling cues or already-visible symbols do not test listening. No universal pass percentage is claimed here.
3. **Production check:** compare your recording with the reference in the same broad context. A listener can report what they heard without seeing the intended answer first. A knowledgeable instructor may evaluate an articulatory contrast more specifically than an untrained listener. Correct word recognition is not proof of every phone feature.
4. **Token-evidence check:** when the task is to transcribe an unknown recording, keep source descriptions separate from your auditory inference. Specialized acoustic analysis can inform voicing, timing or resonance, but no fixed threshold or automatic phone recognizer is supplied. Exact internal tongue configuration generally cannot be uniquely recovered from an ordinary recording alone.
5. Retain uncertainty or request specialist help when the evidence conflicts. A waveform plot, ASR result or throat sensation alone is not a complete articulatory classification.

**Decision points and fallback**
For a familiar symbol with no audio, report “symbol category checked; perception pending; production pending.” For uncertain audio, report a candidate phone and confidence rationale rather than claiming a unique label. For self-listening without another evaluator, record “self-compared only.” These are useful partial outcomes, not failures to conceal.

## Stage6 Record limits and test a new context
**Goal:** Preserve a reusable but bounded analysis and test whether it survives a different example.

**Why:** Correctly identifying one isolated symbol does not establish the realization of every related English phoneme.

**What to do:** Complete the record, apply the analysis to a new word or token, and separate stable category knowledge from contextual variation.

**What to observe:** Changes due to stress, neighboring sounds, speech style, accent or a different transcription convention.

**Success signal:** The record contains actual evidence, pending checks and a justified next decision without generalizing beyond the task.

**Next:** Finish, revisit an earlier stage, or use the result in a separate word analysis; no automatic reciprocal Practice call is required.

**Understanding and procedure**
For instance, after studying `[pʰ]` in an initial stressed context, compare a recorded `/p/` after `/s/` rather than assuming the same aspiration everywhere. After identifying `[z]` from the chart, test another suitable American word; do not assume every written `s` represents `/z/`. With a vowel, retain the distinction between its IPA reference quality and the accent-specific phone heard in the new recording.

Store the original input, source URL or private token identifier, accent, neighboring context, feature bundle, source-supported premises, actual listening observations and your production evidence. Mark any bracketed transcription as **source-provided**, **predicted**, **auditorily inferred** or **measured to a stated extent**. These labels can coexist for different claims; no choice grants blanket certainty. Do not report actual measurements when none were made.

**Decision points and fallback**
If the new case changes the analysis, revise the record and its scope. Publishing reusable knowledge requires a separate evidence review; personal recordings and another person's identity should not enter the public repository by default. Revisit later when you can obtain the missing evidence.

## Evidence status
**Provisional.** The IPA provides source support for symbol classifications and diacritics. The chart inspection did not include audio playback; Sounds American lesson recordings were not played during authoring. Existing source records retain those access limits. The instructional sequence, recording comparisons and success signals are authored proposals, not validated pedagogy. No learner recording, perceptual score, measured acoustic value or expert approval is claimed.
