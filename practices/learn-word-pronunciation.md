# Learn a word pronunciation

## Key takeaway
Learn one General American word by connecting its intended meaning, checked phonemic form, listening evidence, articulatory actions, recorded attempts and a later unprompted check.

## Summary
This Practice integrates scientific analysis with listening and actual production. It produces a **word pronunciation record**, not just an IPA string: spelling and sense, accent and convention, source-supported broad IPA, syllables and stress, context-conditioned sound predictions, recordings or witnessed attempts, feedback and unresolved questions. Analysis, perception and production have separate verification statuses. Use the six stages in the recommended order when starting; prerequisites and missing evidence determine where to enter or return. Reading this document or passing repository tests does not establish pronunciation competence.

## Purpose and prerequisites
Use this for a recurring task such as learning how to say an unfamiliar word in a sentence. General American is the reference, not a demand to copy one speaker or reject other accents. Have the target word in context, a source of American pronunciation with clearly identified provenance, a playback device, and a way to record yourself or obtain feedback from a listener. A mirror is optional. Without audio or a live reference, analysis can proceed but listening and production verification remain **pending**. Without recording, a listener can provide feedback, but retrospective audio comparison is unavailable.

Keep a private record outside the public repository. Record yourself only; obtain permission before recording another person. Link to external lessons and dictionary entries rather than uploading their audio, video or screenshots. Work at a comfortable volume and natural breathing rate. Stop if speaking causes discomfort; do not force articulators to match an illustration.

## Essential distinctions and source roles
A spelling such as `<pin>` is not an instruction for an exact sound. A phonemic form such as `/pɪn/` names contrastive categories. A bracketed form such as `[pʰɪn]` can describe a possible aspirated realization, but it is **predicted**, not observed, unless a recording or live utterance has actually been inspected. A copied IPA symbol is a representation, not itself a phone. Broad versus narrow detail is also different from phonemic versus phonetic analysis.

## Stage1 Define the word and situation
**Goal:** Select one lexical form, meaning and use situation.

**Why:** The same spelling can correspond to different words, senses or stress patterns; a plural or past form is not the uninflected base.

**What to do:** Record spelling, a short meaning, grammatical form, an example sentence and General American as the target.

**What to observe:** Homographs, proper names, abbreviations and differences between saying a word alone and in connected speech.

**Success signal:** A second reader could identify which word form and use you intend without guessing.

**Next:** Obtain a candidate transcription; clarify the target first if the context is ambiguous.

**Understanding and procedure**
1. Write an original sentence, such as “Please pass the pin.” State that *pin* means a small fastener, rather than an abbreviation or a different item.
2. Preserve the exact word form. If the target is *pins*, record the plural; if it is *record*, identify whether the intended use is a noun or a verb before looking up pronunciation.
3. State the initial goal: a recognizable word in isolation and in this sentence. Native-like accent, exact acoustic imitation and speaking faster are not default success requirements.
4. Set up separate fields for the proposed transcription, actual source observations and your own attempts. The separation prevents an expected feature from being reported as heard.

**Decision points and fallback**
If a name or unfamiliar technical term has conflicting pronunciations, retain alternatives and consult a suitable speaker or domain-specific source. Do not force an ordinary spelling rule onto it. An unresolved target is a reason to pause, not to fabricate certainty.

## Stage2 Establish a candidate phonemic form
**Goal:** Obtain and check a broad General American representation.

**Why:** A converter supplies a candidate lexical analysis; it does not observe how a person pronounced the word.

**What to do:** Select American in toPhonetics, inspect alternatives and settings, then compare the appropriate dictionary entry.

**What to observe:** Sense mismatch, an unrecognized word, different transcription conventions, weak forms and unresolved accent differences.

**Success signal:** The chosen form has a stated source and convention, or competing candidates remain explicitly unresolved.

**Next:** Listen to an appropriate reference; return to Stage1 for a wrong sense or form.

**Understanding and procedure**
1. Enter the word with enough sentence context to identify the intended use. Record the American setting and whether “Show weak forms” is enabled. For a citation-form goal, begin without intentionally selecting a weak form; later check the phrase separately.
2. Inspect any alternate readings. Consult a dictionary for the intended sense and inflection; do not select the first option merely because it is offered.
3. Record the original notation before normalizing it. In this package, `/i/` and `/u/` are common GA broad conventions, whereas another source may retain length marks. A notation difference alone is not proof of a pronunciation difference. Consult the source's key rather than relabeling dictionary respelling as IPA.
4. Record the source URL and actual date and extent of inspection. If no suitable corroborating source is available, label the candidate provisional.

**Decision points and fallback**
Do not resolve differing vowels, rhotics or stress by blind symbol substitution. Check accent, lexical sense, notation and recording context first. A disagreement can remain unresolved. Reference-token playback is not a requirement for proposing broad IPA, but it is necessary for later listening checks.

## Stage3 Listen for segments syllables and stress
**Goal:** Connect the lexical analysis to an actual audible reference.

**Why:** Recognizing a written sequence does not demonstrate hearing its syllables, contrasts or prominence.

**What to do:** Listen without looking first, note what you perceive, then replay while comparing with the proposed transcription.

**What to observe:** Missing or additional syllables, difficult vowel contrasts, consonant sequence, lexical stress and possible reduction.

**Success signal:** You can align an inspected recording or live demonstration with the intended word and identify the remaining uncertainties.

**Next:** Work on the uncertain sounds and assemble the word; obtain an accessible recording if listening is unavailable.

**Understanding and procedure**
1. Choose a clearly labeled American reference with a known source. Prefer an identified human pronunciation for observation of a human token; identify synthesized playback as **TTS** when used. A tool's American transcription setting does not guarantee the browser voice or waveform matches it.
2. Listen at ordinary speed without IPA or spelling and write the heard syllable count and any uncertain sound. Then compare with the lexical entry and replay. Slow playback may help inspection, but return to normal speed; altered playback can change temporal or spectral cues.
3. For a multisyllabic word, identify the stressed syllable. In the illustrative `/əˈbaʊt/`, primary stress belongs to `/baʊt/`. Stress is not simply loudness, and `/aʊ/` can occupy one syllable nucleus despite using two symbols.
4. Where a contrast is genuinely uncertain, compare a suitable pair from the same accent. For instance, *pin* and *bin* isolate a lexical contrast; their stops must not be judged only by the presence or absence of a throat buzz.
5. Hide the labels and ask a partner to present a short shuffled set of known reference words. Record the actual choices and uncertainty; predictable alternation or reading labels is not listening verification.

**Decision points and fallback**
If sources are inaccessible, record “listening pending.” Do not infer that you heard aspiration merely because the word normally permits it. Exact syllable boundaries and phonetic details can remain analysis-dependent. Seek a better sample or informed feedback rather than repeating an uncertain guess.

## Stage4 Shape the sounds and assemble the word
**Goal:** Relate an articulatory plan to a comfortable production of the whole word.

**Why:** Separate phoneme names are not the same thing as coordinated speech movements.

**What to do:** Inspect the relevant sound lesson, practice the difficult gesture, then join it into its syllable and word without extra vowels.

**What to observe:** Lip and tongue placement, constriction, airflow, vowel quality, smooth transitions and unintended segment insertion.

**Success signal:** You can produce a plausible target comfortably and explain the gesture being attempted, while leaving its accuracy for comparison.

**Next:** Record and compare; return to the reference if the gesture or target is uncertain.

**Understanding and procedure**
1. In Sounds American, find the sound lesson corresponding to the difficult segment; use its explanation and demonstration together. Record the actual lesson and playback position you consulted. The authoring of this Practice does not claim that those videos were played.
2. For consonants, identify place (where), manner (how airflow is constrained) and laryngeal behavior. For vowels, identify height, backness, rounding and any movement. Work on one contrast at a time rather than exaggerating every property.
3. In the constructed *pin* example, close and release both lips for the initial bilabial stop, move into the `/ɪ/` vowel and finish with the alveolar nasal `/n/`. A stressed initial `/p/` commonly has aspiration. That expectation does not make `[pʰ]` an observed fact about your attempt. Do not add a separate “uh” after the stop or final nasal.
4. Practice a brief sound gesture, then the syllable, the word and the chosen sentence. A stop is not sustained like a fricative; a diphthong is not two independently stressed vowels. When normal production differs from the most careful model, investigate the context before declaring an error.

**Decision points and fallback**
Stop if speaking causes discomfort. A mirror can show lips but not prove an internal tongue position. If the uncertainty is about the sound category itself, use the self-contained [Classify and produce a phone](classify-and-produce-phone.md) Practice, then return here. That optional sub-Practice has no requirement to call this word Practice, so the procedure does not recurse indefinitely.

## Stage5 Record compare and diagnose
**Goal:** Collect evidence about your performance instead of relying on familiarity.

**Why:** Knowing the intended word can bias self-listening, and automatic transcription can recover words despite sound differences.

**What to do:** Record the word and its sentence, alternate reference and attempt playback, and obtain a listener's independent response when possible.

**What to observe:** Segment identity, syllable count, lexical stress, contextual realization and what the listener actually understood.

**Success signal:** The record distinguishes successful checks, identified differences and unverified features, rather than saying only “sounds correct.”

**Next:** Revise one diagnosed issue, or move to unprompted retrieval when the available checks support doing so.

**Understanding and procedure**
1. Make a short ordinary-volume recording in a reasonably quiet environment. Preserve a baseline attempt and a later attempt instead of keeping only the best one. Use original neutral sentences, without personal information.
2. Listen reference–attempt–reference. Compare one dimension at a time: syllable count, stress, vowel contrast, consonant gesture, then connected transitions. Do not demand identical pitch or waveform shape from different speakers.
3. Ask a listener familiar with the target variety to identify the word without showing it first, then comment on the particular contrast. A sentence offers context, so separately check the word or a neutral carrier phrase when practical. Write the listener's actual response; do not substitute your expectation.
4. Self-comparison is useful but remains **self-checked**. A listener's recognition supports intelligibility in that task, not an exact narrow transcription or articulatory diagnosis. Speech-recognition output or an app score is supplementary evidence, not sufficient proof of pronunciation accuracy.
5. Change one plausible source of error and repeat the same bounded task. If no listener or recording is available, retain a partial result and specify what was not tested.

**Decision points and fallback**
A different GA variant may be legitimate. First check lexical contrast and evidence, not whether your production matches one person's accent precisely. Persistent uncertainty calls for a qualified pronunciation teacher or phonetician when exact realization matters; no acoustic or clinical diagnosis is made here.

## Stage6 Retrieve transfer and retain evidence
**Goal:** Check whether the word can be recovered and used without immediate imitation.

**Why:** A successful echoed attempt does not establish independent recall or use in a new sentence.

**What to do:** Hide IPA and audio, produce the word from its meaning, use a new original sentence, and schedule a later self-check in your own record.

**What to observe:** Dependence on the prompt, return of an earlier error, and differences between isolated and contextual production.

**Success signal:** Analysis, perception and production have explicit evidence statuses, with a repeatable next check and no invented outcome.

**Next:** Retain the record, repeat the relevant stage, or seek feedback; do not equate one session with mastery.

**Understanding and procedure**
Use the meaning or a picture you have permission to use as a cue. Say the word before revealing its spelling or replaying the model, then place it in a different sentence. Check the same features as before and record the result. Repeat on a later occasion chosen by you; this Practice makes no claim about an optimal interval or validated number of repetitions.

Use separate labels: **analysis supported or unresolved**, **perception tested or pending**, **production self-checked or listener-corroborated**, and **later transfer checked or pending**. The combined option is not complete merely because an IPA form was found. A missing listening or production check stays visible. Even all four favorable labels support only the recorded task, not a general proficiency certificate.

**Decision points and fallback**
If independent retrieval fails, return to the earliest uncertain stage. Keep alternative pronunciations and the basis for your choice. Store recordings and personal results outside the public repository; a public knowledge change should contain only appropriately authorized, de-identified evidence and an explicit review scope.

**Provisional.** This is an authored integration procedure, not an experimentally validated learning intervention. Proposed checks and revisit decisions are design choices. Selected sources support notation, sound categories, aspiration and tool descriptions; they do not establish this Practice's effectiveness, any learner's ability or every constructed example.
