## Definition

Common ground is information participants mutually treat as accepted for the purposes of an interaction in a working discourse model. It need not be objectively true or all currently salient. Accepted background and momentary attention are related but distinct.

## Worked example

An assertion "The meeting begins at four" proposes an update; a reply "No, at five" blocks straightforward mutual acceptance. "Maya's brother called" may lead a hearer to accommodate that Maya has a brother, or to ask which brother rather than accept every detail.

## Distinctions

Assertion does not automatically become common ground. Accommodation is possible, not compulsory. Mutual acceptance in a hypothetical discussion need not express the participants' real-world beliefs.

## Scope and limits

This note adopts a pragmatic documentation model without committing to a precise hierarchy of mutual belief. Conversation analysis and formal dynamic semantics offer different detailed accounts.
