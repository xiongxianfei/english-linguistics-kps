# Vowel articulation

## Key takeaway
Vowel descriptions commonly use height, backness and lip rounding.

## Summary
Vowel descriptions commonly use height, backness and lip rounding. The definitions and worked cases below state the intended analysis and its limits. This initial object is provisional; links deepen the explanation but do not replace it.

## Definition

Vowel descriptions commonly use height, backness and lip rounding. English lexical vowels can be mapped to approximate IPA qualities, but a broad phonemic symbol should not be mistaken for an exact cardinal-vowel target across accents.

## Worked example

Primary General American broad labels: /i/ close front unrounded (FLEECE), /u/ conventionally close and rounded (GOOSE), /æ/ near-open front unrounded (TRAP), and /ə/ central, commonly unstressed (the first syllable of *about*). The earlier /iː/ and /uː/ spellings illustrate a British-style broad convention and are not the new default. These IPA categories are not identical to one measured American speaker's vowel targets.

## General American transcription and source scope

New word examples use the General American convention defined in [PRONUNCIATION](../docs/PRONUNCIATION.md). toPhonetics can suggest a broad word transcription but cannot establish its acoustic vowel position. Sounds American helps explain instructional articulation; the International Phonetic Association chart identifies general phonetic symbols. GOOSE fronting, cot and caught mergers, schwa reduction and contextual vowel duration need separate accent-sensitive treatment. The absence of /ː/ in a conventional phonemic transcription does not imply identical phonetic durations.

## Distinctions

Low does not mean rounded. Front does not logically entail unrounded across all languages. Contemporary English GOOSE vowels may be fronted, and vowel duration is not determined solely by the length mark.

## Scope and limits

Exact qualities, diphthongization and reduction vary by accent and context. No acoustic measurement or judgment of a user's pronunciation is claimed. The table is a simplified reference, not an accent prescription.
