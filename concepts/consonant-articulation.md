# Consonant articulation

## Key takeaway
Consonants can be classified by voicing, place and manner of articulation.

## Summary
Consonants can be classified by voicing, place and manner of articulation. The definitions and worked cases below state the intended analysis and its limits. This initial object is provisional; links deepen the explanation but do not replace it.

## Definition

Consonants can be classified by voicing, place and manner of articulation. These dimensions describe laryngeal activity, constriction location and airflow configuration. A conventional phonemic label is not a guarantee of identical voicing in every token.

## Worked example

Introductory feature bundles: /p/ voiceless bilabial stop; /z/ voiced alveolar fricative; /ŋ/ voiced velar nasal. /s/ contrasts with /z/ principally in the laryngeal dimension. /θ/ and /ð/ are dental fricatives, distinct from alveolar /s/ and /z/.

## Distinctions

English nasals /m n ŋ/ share nasal manner but have different places. /ŋ/ is not an approximant. An affricate such as /tʃ/ has a coordinated closure and fricated release rather than being classified by its two letters.

## Scope and limits

Actual English stop contrasts use cues including voice onset timing, aspiration and duration; /b/ need not be fully voiced in every position. Articulatory diagrams and listener judgments require accent-specific evidence.
