## Definition

A presupposition is content treated as backgrounded in a use or associated with a triggering construction in a theory. Projection through negation is useful evidence, but not an exceptionless test. Backgrounded does not mean previously known by every hearer.

## Worked example

"Maya stopped working here" normally takes earlier work at the relevant place for granted. "Maya did not stop working here" often retains that assumption. The positive sentence describes cessation at the relevant time; it need not guarantee that Maya never resumed work later.

## Distinctions

Assertion proposes foregrounded content; presupposition can be accommodated or challenged. "Maya knows that Ravi left" behaves factively in ordinary uses, unlike thinks, but embedding and local context can affect projection.

## Scope and limits

The source record notes competing semantic and pragmatic approaches. An entailment may also project; a simplistic three-column checklist cannot mechanically classify all inferences.
